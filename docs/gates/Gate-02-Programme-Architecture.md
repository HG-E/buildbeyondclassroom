# Build Beyond Classroom: Programme Architecture (Gate 2)

Oct 13, 2026 · Ekene

## What Gate 1 locked in

Gate 2 turns the Gate 1 brief into a structure people can enrol in. It rests on four things from that brief and on three assumptions that you can overturn.

- **Locked from Gate 1:** the four musts (build with AI, find and validate a community problem, make the business case in your own field, own the result), the seven-rung capability ladder, the three levels (Understand and use, Apply and build, Create and scale), and the Community Problem Studio as the signature method.
- **Assumption A:** one shared core serves every learner, and what differs between learners is the journey around it, not the core itself.
- **Assumption B:** the unit of delivery is a project cohort, because Gate 1 flagged completion as the largest risk and online-only courses lose most learners.
- **Assumption C:** the legal form (non-profit, social enterprise or hybrid) is still open, so nothing here depends on it.

Nothing in this document sets prices, durations or enrolment targets. Those wait for the pilot design in Gate 4.

Learners enter one of three journeys by level; all of them use the same Studio core and field lens, produce evidence they own, and are supervised by certified local facilitators.

## Learner journeys

There are three learner journeys, matched to the three levels, plus one educator route covered below. A learner enters at the level that matches what they can already build, not at the level their age or year suggests.

| Journey | Who it is for | Level and ladder rungs | Studio focus | Leaves with |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Explore | Pre-university learners, about to choose a course or career | 1. Understand and use (rungs 1–3) | A small problem close to home; the field lens used to sample several fields | A first AI-built prototype and a clear view of how a chosen field links to real problems and work |
| Build | University and college students | 2. Apply and build (rungs 3–6) | A problem found through their own course or campus; built, deployed and piloted with real users | A deployed solution, a portfolio entry and an internship-ready business case for their field |
| Launch | Graduates, interns and early founders | 3. Create and scale (rungs 5–7) | Taking an existing solution into a second community, with a funding or revenue path | A solution adapted in a second community, a verified portfolio and a route to funding or employment |

All three journeys share the same four stages, so a facilitator can run mixed groups:

1. **Orient.** A readiness build task sets the starting level, tools are chosen under the age and cost rules, and the project rules are agreed.
2. **Studio cycle.** The team works through the eight Community Problem Studio steps at its level.
3. **Showcase and verify.** Learners demonstrate the project to the community partner and complete a short unaided check.
4. **Next step.** Learners move up a level, mentor a newer cohort, or hand the project to its named local owner.

The readiness build task is a proposal: it should be piloted, because an age-blind entry rule is only fair if the task itself is.

## Field lens: the business side of your own classroom

Every learner studies the business side of their own field through one fixed set of questions, so a law student, a farmer's child and a nursing student all learn entrepreneurship from their own ground. The lens runs alongside the Studio cycle at every level; only its depth changes (value and customer, then model and pilot, then scale and sustainability).

The five lens questions:

1. **Who benefits** from the solution, and who else is affected?
2. **Who pays**, now and in future: users, institutions, funders or no one?
3. **What it costs** to build, run and keep safe.
4. **What rules apply** in the field: professional standards, ethics, licensing and data law.
5. **Who already serves this need**, and what would make ours worth adding?

The table shows how the same lens plays out in different fields. These are illustrations of the method, not recommended projects; real projects come from the community research step.

| Field | A problem a learner might research | Who might pay or sponsor | Field rule the learner must check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Agriculture | Crop and market advice for smallholders in a local language | Cooperatives, NGOs, local government | Accuracy of advice; who is liable when it is wrong |
| Health sciences | Plain-language health information for a clinic's visitors | Clinics, public health bodies | Professional and data rules; no diagnosis by AI |
| Law | A guide to everyday legal rights for a neighbourhood | Legal-aid groups, foundations | Rules on legal advice; keeping facts current |
| Education | A tutoring or revision aid for a school | Schools, parents' associations | Child data and consent rules |
| Arts and media | Local-language content for a community or small business | Small businesses, cultural bodies | Copyright and consent for sources |

The lens is also the programme's career bridge: after a cycle, a learner can state what their field looks like as a business and where they could work in it, which serves the pre-university and internship-stage learners the programme is built for.

## Educator and facilitator track

The programme scales through local facilitators, not through a central team, so this route is part of the architecture from day one. Gate 1 named facilitator capacity as a kill-criterion: if facilitators cannot run a cohort unaided by cycle 2, rollout pauses.

The route has three steps:

1. **Participate.** Facilitators first take the Build journey themselves, so they have shipped a project and know where learners get stuck.
2. **Co-run.** They run a cohort alongside an experienced facilitator, using the open playbook and the Studio toolkit (Gate 3).
3. **Lead and certify.** They run a cohort on their own, are observed, and receive the facilitator credential, which also licenses use of the studio locally.

Facilitators include teachers, lecturers, community-hub leaders and NGO staff. They are also the supervising adults for younger learners (see the age and access bands), so the credential covers child-data and safeguarding practice, not only teaching method.

Facilitator certification is also one of the revenue streams in the Gate 1 business model, so training quality affects both impact and funding.

## Assessment, credentials and portfolio

A level is earned by evidence of what the learner built and can do unaided, never by attendance. This follows the Gate 1 finding that gains held when AI tutored and faded when AI wrote for students.

| Evidence | What it proves | Who verifies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shipped project | The learner built and deployed something that works | Facilitator, against the level's output description |
| Community confirmation | Real people used it and the need was real | The community partner |
| Unaided check | The learner can explain and extend the work without the tool | Facilitator, at the start, middle and end of a cycle |
| Field business case | The learner applied the five lens questions to their own field | Facilitator, with a field expert where one is available |
| 90-day follow-up | The solution is still in use, with a named local owner | Programme team, from the partner's report |

The portfolio holds the project, the business case, the community confirmation and the unaided-check results, and belongs to the learner. It is the main output; a credential is only a summary of it.

Two rules keep the credential honest:

- It describes itself as a programme credential, not an accredited qualification, until a university or ministry partner recognises it. Credit recognition is a goal for Gate 8 (partnerships).
- It records the level reached and the project evidence behind it, so employers and universities can look at the work itself.

## Age and access bands

Age never decides whether someone can learn here; it decides who supervises, which tools they use and how real money and data are handled. The bands below are a working design, and local counsel must confirm them in each country before launch, because child-data law differs: India's DPDP Act treats everyone under 18 as a child, while GDPR sets the consent age between 13 and 16 by member state.

| Band | How they take part | Supervision | Tools and data | Business side |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Under 13 | Facilitator-led sessions only | Facilitator present throughout; parent or educator consent | Facilitator-held accounts or offline tools; no personal data collected | Concepts and mock ventures |
| 13–15 | Cohort with a facilitator | Facilitator and parent or educator consent | Tools only where the vendor's age rule allows it, otherwise facilitator-run; minimal data | Mock ventures, or real ones with guardian awareness |
| 16–17 | Cohort or hub | Facilitator; guardian informed | Own accounts where permitted; minimal data | Real community pilots with guardian awareness |
| 18 and over | Cohort, hub or self-directed with a mentor | Mentor or facilitator | Own accounts; the learner controls their data | Real pilots, revenue and funding applications |

Three rules apply in every band:

- **Strictest standard wins.** Where a site is covered by more than one rule, the stricter one applies.
- **No tracking or profiling of learners.** Collect only what the project needs.
- **Tool age limits are re-checked every cohort.** Chatbot age rules differ by vendor and by country and change often, so no tool is built into the core.

## Decisions needed and next gate

Four decisions shape Gate 3 (the Community Problem Studio method and toolkit). My recommendation is in bold.

1. **Entry rule:** place learners by a readiness build task rather than by age or school year. **Recommended: yes, and pilot the task.**
2. **Journey names:** keep Explore, Build and Launch as working names, and test them with partners in several languages, as with the programme name.
3. **Credential wording:** call it a programme credential until a partner recognises it. **Recommended: yes.**
4. **Age bands:** accept the four bands as the working design, subject to counsel in each pilot country. **Recommended: yes.**

Still open from Gate 1 and not blocking Gate 3: legal form, the three pilot contexts, a second delivery lead and the domain and trademark checks.
