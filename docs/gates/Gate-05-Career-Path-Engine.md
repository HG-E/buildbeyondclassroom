# Build Beyond Classroom: Career Path Engine (Gate 5)

Oct 14, 2026 · Ekene

## What the engine is, and what it is not

The career path engine helps a learner see how their course of study or interest connects to real fields, roles and next steps, using the business side of that field as the bridge. It serves the pre-university learner choosing a course, the student looking for an internship, and the graduate or early founder choosing a first role or venture.

It is a structured library of field descriptions plus a guided method for using it, not a prediction machine. Three limits are built in from the start:

- **It does not tell anyone what to become.** It shows options and evidence, and the learner decides, with a mentor or parent where they want one.
- **It does not promise outcomes.** No income, placement or admission claims appear anywhere in it.
- **It does not rank or profile learners.** It collects only what the learner chooses to enter, and scores no one.

It works at three levels of technology. At the lowest level it is a set of printed Field Cards and a conversation guide, which a facilitator can run with no device. At the middle level it is a shared spreadsheet or form. At the highest level it is a web tool, specified in Gate 7. All three use the same content.

The learner brings a course or interest, their level and place, and their Studio evidence. The engine combines the universal Field Card with the local layer, and the learner leaves with a plan they own, reviewed in a conversation with a facilitator or mentor.

## The Field Card

Every field the programme covers is described on one standard Field Card, so a learner can compare fields side by side and a new country can add its own cards without redesigning the engine. Cards are written by facilitators with field experts, and each section is either universal or local.

| Card section | What it answers | Layer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The field in one paragraph | What community need does this field serve? | Universal |
| How value is made and paid for | Who benefits, who pays, and what it costs: the five lens questions applied to the field | Universal, with local examples |
| Typical roles | What work do people do in it, as employees, self-employed people, founders, public servants or NGO staff? Roles are tagged to a public occupation code for comparison across countries | Universal |
| How AI is changing the work | Which tasks AI now supports and which it does not; dated and sourced | Universal, reviewed regularly |
| Skills ladder | Which skills matter at Level 1, 2 and 3, and which capability rungs they use | Universal |
| Rules of the field | Licensing, professional bodies, ethics and data rules | Local |
| Entry routes | Degree, diploma, apprenticeship, internship, certificate or self-employment | Local |
| Studio project ideas | Illustrative problems and likely community partners | Universal, with local examples |
| Local opportunities | Employers, internship hosts, programmes and regulators in the learner's area | Local |
| Review record | Who wrote the card, who checked it, the sources used and the date | Both |

Two rules keep the cards honest. Pay or demand figures appear only when taken from a published local source and dated, and a card is marked out of date when its review is overdue. A card with no named reviewer is not used with learners.

## One engine, three learner questions

The same cards serve all three journeys from Gate 2. What changes is the question the learner brings and the output they leave with.

| Journey | The question they bring | What the engine does | Output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Explore (pre-university) | Which course or field should I choose? | The learner reads several Field Cards, compares how each field makes and spends value, and tries a small Studio task in the field that interests them most | A one-page Field Comparison and a shortlist of courses, with questions to ask each admissions office |
| Build (university and college) | How do I turn my course into an internship or first job? | The learner takes their own field's card, compares its skills ladder with their portfolio, and finds the gaps | An Internship Plan: target hosts, the project to show, and the skills to add next |
| Launch (graduates, interns, early founders) | Should I look for a role or start a venture? | The learner compares employment and venture routes using the card's value model and entry routes | A Next-Step Plan: a role search with evidence, or a venture plan with funding paths |

Learners who change field use two cards and compare them. Learners with no clear interest start from the community problems they found in Studio step 1 and see which fields work on them, which often shows a field they had not considered.

Each output is reviewed in a short conversation with a facilitator or mentor. The conversation guide gives the questions to ask and the questions to avoid, such as asking a learner to justify a choice that a family or a school has already made for them.

## From portfolio to internship

A Studio project is the strongest thing a student can bring to an internship search, because it shows a real problem, a working solution and a named community reference. The engine turns that project into an application in five steps.

1. **Translate the project into the field's language.** The learner restates the project using the Field Card's roles and skills, so an employer in that field recognises it.
2. **Write the one-page case.** The learner summarises the need, the evidence, the solution, the result and the business case, with the partner's confirmation attached.
3. **Find hosts.** The learner lists internship hosts from the Field Card's local opportunities and from the programme's partner network, starting with community partners and employers who sponsored challenges.
4. **Prepare for the conversation.** The learner rehearses explaining the project without the tool, using the unaided checks from the pilot as practice.
5. **Apply and record.** The learner applies, records what happened, and brings the result back to the facilitator. Rejections are data, and the card is updated if it was wrong.

Two things sit outside the programme's control and are stated honestly to learners: the programme cannot guarantee a placement, and many hosts will have formal application rules that the learner must follow. The programme's part is to make the learner's evidence clear and verifiable, and to build employer relationships through the community and employer challenge stream in the Gate 1 business model.

## Evidence and fairness rules

Career advice goes wrong when it is out of date, copied from another country or quietly biased. These rules apply to every card and every output.

### Where the structure comes from

The cards borrow their vocabulary from three public occupation sources, so roles can be compared across countries without the programme inventing its own system:

- [ESCO](https://employment-social-affairs.ec.europa.eu/policies-and-activities/skills-and-qualifications/skills-jobs/european-skillscompetences-qualifications-and-occupations-esco_en), the European Commission's multilingual classification of occupations and skills, free to use and available in 28 languages.
- [ISCO-08](https://unstats.un.org/unsd/classifications/Family/Detail/1067/), the International Labour Organization's classification, with four levels from 10 major groups to 436 unit groups, built so that occupational data can be compared across countries.
- [O*NET](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/eta/onet), the US Department of Labor's database of tasks, skills and abilities by occupation, updated annually.

These describe occupations as defined in their own regions. O*NET in particular reflects the US economy, so it is used only for task and skill descriptions and never for local demand. Work that has no clean code, including much informal and self-employed work and newer AI-related roles, is described in plain words on the card, not forced into a code.

### Rules for the content

1. **Every factual claim carries a source and a date.** A claim without both is removed.
2. **Local facts come from local sources:** national statistics, professional bodies, employers and the community's own partners.
3. **No income or placement promises.** Pay appears only as a dated range from a published source.
4. **A named human reviewer signs each card.** AI may help draft a card, but it never reviews its own draft.
5. **Cards expire.** Each has a review date, and an overdue card is marked and withdrawn from use.

### Rules for fairness and data

- **Check who is being steered where.** Reviewers look at whether the engine's suggestions differ by gender, region or income without a reason, and fix the cards if they do.
- **Show options, not scores.** No learner is ranked, rated or labelled.
- **Keep data minimal.** The learner controls what they enter. Nothing about a learner's choices is shared with a school, employer or funder without their consent, and the Gate 2 child-data rules apply to every learner under 18.
- **AI is a tutor here too.** Learners may use AI to explore a card and ask questions about it. They do not use it to decide, and facilitators do not use it to judge a learner's prospects.

## Build plan

The engine is built in two versions, so the pilot can test the content before any software is written. Building a web tool first would risk polishing the wrong thing.

| Version | What it is | What it needs | What it tests |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0: paper and spreadsheet | Printed Field Cards for the fields the pilot cohort studies, a conversation guide and a shared spreadsheet for the outputs | Field experts as reviewers, the card template, and a facilitator trained to use the guide | Whether learners can use a card to make a real decision, and whether the cards are accurate |
| 1: web tool | The same cards and outputs as a searchable, multilingual tool with a low-data mode and printable pages | The Gate 7 platform specification, a content team and a review process | Whether it reaches learners beyond the pilot site and how often cards go out of date |

For the pilot, write cards only for the fields the cohort's learners actually study, and add more once the first cards have been used and corrected. Every card used in the pilot is logged with the corrections learners and reviewers made, and those corrections are the main evidence for whether the engine is worth building as software.

The pilot adds two engine measures to the Gate 4 list: how many learners finish an output (a Field Comparison, Internship Plan or Next-Step Plan), and how many cards needed material correction after first use. Targets for both are set with the evaluator before week 1.

## Decisions needed and next gate

Four decisions shape Gate 6. My recommendation is in bold.

1. **Which cards to write first:** only the fields the pilot cohort studies, then add more after the first corrections. **Recommended: yes.**
2. **Who reviews a card:** a facilitator drafts it and a named field expert checks it, from a partner university, professional body or employer. **Recommended: yes, and no card is used without a named reviewer.**
3. **Vocabulary:** tag roles with ESCO and ISCO-08 where they fit, use O*NET only for task and skill descriptions, and describe uncoded work in plain words. **Recommended: yes.**
4. **Engine version for the pilot:** version 0 on paper and spreadsheet, with the web tool left to Gate 7 and later. **Recommended: yes.**

Still open from earlier gates: legal form, the pilot site and context, the second delivery lead, the three Gate 3 and Gate 4 confirmations, and the domain and trademark checks.

Next gate: Gate 6, the delivery model: how the programme runs physically, virtually and in hybrid form, including hubs, facilitator ratios, connectivity tiers, costs and what each mode needs. I will start it when you say "start".
