# Build Beyond Classroom: Community Problem Studio (Gate 3)

Oct 13, 2026 · Ekene

## What Gate 3 delivers

The Community Problem Studio is the method every learner and facilitator runs, whatever their level, field or country. This gate turns the eight steps from Gate 1 into a working method: what happens in each step, what the team must produce, how AI is used, which templates and tools support it, and how a facilitator runs one cycle.

Five rules hold in every cycle, and every later gate must keep them:

1. **The community owns the problem.** Learners do not invent a need. They find it by listening to real people and confirm it with a named community partner.
2. **Evidence comes before building.** No team starts building until it can show who has the problem, how often, and what they do about it today.
3. **AI is a tutor and co-builder, not the author.** Learners use it to research, draft, build and test, then verify and explain the work without it.
4. **The field lens runs through every step.** The five questions from Gate 2 (who benefits, who pays, what it costs, what rules apply, who else serves the need) are answered as the work goes, not at the end.
5. **Every project ends with an owner.** A named local person or organisation takes it over, or the team documents why it stops.

The method is an original synthesis for this programme. It has not been tested yet, so Gate 4 treats the pilot as the test and reports which steps held up. Nothing here sets durations, team sizes or numeric targets; those come with the pilot design.

## The eight steps at a glance

Each step asks one question, ends with one output the team keeps in its portfolio, and closes with an exit check. A team moves on only when the check is passed, or when the facilitator and team agree to loop back or stop.

| Step | The question it answers | Team output | Exit check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Find the need | What problem do real people in our community face? | Need Note: who has the problem, how they cope today, and the community partner's name | The partner confirms the problem in their own words |
| 2. Research feasibility | Is it real, big enough and doable by us? | Evidence Pack: interviews and observations, existing solutions, limits on data, law, cost and connectivity; first answers to the five lens questions | Facilitator and team make a go, redesign or stop call, with reasons |
| 3. Ideate | What could solve it, and which option is worth building? | Options list, the chosen idea with the reason, and a first idea of how it could scale | The team explains why this option beat the others |
| 4. Design | What exactly will we build, and for whom? | Solution Spec: users, flow, data used, what AI does and does not do, and how success will be measured | The partner reacts to the spec or a sketch and the do-no-harm check is started |
| 5. Build | Does a working version exist? | Working prototype and a build log of prompts, versions and fixes | The main user flow works, and each member can explain the parts unaided |
| 6. Deploy | Is it in real hands, safely? | A live pilot with real users, a test report and the do-no-harm check completed | Real users finish the main task and the safety check is passed |
| 7. Pitch | Can we show its value and ask for what it needs? | A pitch and a one-page business case with the lens answers finished | The partner and a panel give feedback, and an unaided check is done |
| 8. Sustain | Who keeps it alive, and how will we know it works? | Sustain Plan: named owner, running costs, maintenance, the 90-day measure, and a handover or stop note | The owner accepts the handover in writing |

Step 8 feeds the next cycle: what the team learned about the community is the starting point for step 1 of the next project.

## Steps 1 to 4: from need to design

These four steps decide whether the project deserves to be built, so they carry the most facilitator attention.

### 1. Find the need

- **Learners do:** observe and talk to people in the places their field touches (a clinic, farm, school, market, court or workshop), keep a listening log, and shortlist problems that people raised themselves.
- **AI helps with:** drafting interview questions, grouping notes into themes, and challenging the team's assumptions. It does not tell the team what the community needs.
- **Watch for:** a problem the team finds interesting rather than one people have, and a solution disguised as a problem ("they need an app").
- **Facilitator move:** ask "who told you this, and how do you know?"

### 2. Research feasibility

- **Learners do:** talk to people who have the problem and people who serve it today, map existing solutions, check limits on data, law, cost and connectivity, and write first answers to the five lens questions.
- **AI helps with:** summarising public sources, comparing options and planning a survey. Every figure it gives is traced to a source the learner has opened.
- **Watch for:** confirmation bias, and statistics that AI has invented.
- **Facilitator move:** require an explicit go, redesign or stop call, with reasons. Stopping is a valid, creditable outcome.

### 3. Ideate

- **Learners do:** generate options with and without AI, including options that need no software, test each against the evidence, choose one, and state what would have to be true for it to work in a second community.
- **AI helps with:** widening the option list, acting as a critic, and finding similar solutions elsewhere.
- **Watch for:** defaulting to an app when a simpler fix, such as a checklist or a message group, would solve it.
- **Facilitator move:** ask "what is the smallest thing that would help?"

### 4. Design

- **Learners do:** write the Solution Spec, sketch the user flow, decide what AI does and what a human does, list the data used and why, agree a success measure with the partner, and start the do-no-harm check.
- **AI helps with:** turning sketches into mock-ups, drafting the spec for the team to correct, and suggesting edge cases.
- **Watch for:** over-scoping, and collecting data the solution does not need.
- **Facilitator move:** cut the spec to the one user flow that proves the idea.

## Steps 5 to 8: from build to handover

These steps are where learners climb the capability ladder, so the tools change by level, but the outputs and checks stay the same.

### 5. Build

- **Learners do:** build the one user flow from the spec, using the rungs their level allows (no-code and AI app builders at Level 1 and 2, automation, assistants, code with AI help and deployment at Levels 2 and 3), and keep a build log of prompts, versions and fixes.
- **AI helps with:** generating drafts, code and content, explaining errors, and testing. Learners review every output before it is used.
- **Watch for:** accepting output the team cannot explain, and building features outside the spec.
- **Facilitator move:** pick a part at random and ask the builder to explain it with the tool closed.

### 6. Deploy

- **Learners do:** put the solution in front of real users through a channel they already use (a web page, a messaging channel, a printed guide with an AI-built back end), run the test report, and complete the do-no-harm check.
- **AI helps with:** writing test cases and user instructions, and spotting weak points.
- **Watch for:** deploying before the safety check, and real user data collected without a reason or consent.
- **Facilitator move:** the facilitator signs off the safety check; the team cannot self-certify.

### 7. Pitch

- **Learners do:** present the problem, the evidence, the solution and its results to the partner and a panel, with a one-page business case that finishes the five lens answers for their own field.
- **AI helps with:** rehearsal, questions from a mock panel and tidying slides. The team writes the argument.
- **Watch for:** claims beyond the evidence, and a pitch the presenters cannot defend in questions.
- **Facilitator move:** the unaided check happens here; each member answers questions without the tool.

### 8. Sustain

- **Learners do:** agree the handover with a named owner, estimate running costs and upkeep, set the 90-day measure, and write the Sustain Plan. If the project stops, they write the stop note and what was learned.
- **AI helps with:** costing scenarios, maintenance checklists and a plain-language user guide.
- **Watch for:** no named owner, and a solution that depends on one learner's account or device.
- **Facilitator move:** do not close the cycle until the owner accepts in writing, or the stop note is filed.

## AI use rules

Learners use AI in three modes, and the programme sets which mode is allowed where. The rule rests on a July 2026 experiment, cited in the Gate 1 brief, in which gains held when AI tutored and faded when AI wrote for students.

| Mode | What AI does | Where it is used | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tutor | Explains, asks questions, critiques the team's work | Every step | Always allowed |
| Co-builder | Drafts text, code, mock-ups and test cases | Mainly steps 4 to 6 | Allowed if the learner reviews, edits and can explain the result |
| Author | Writes the argument, the evidence reading or the reflection | Steps 2, 7 and 8 | Not allowed: these are the learner's own thinking |

Four habits make the rules checkable:

- **Build log.** Learners record the prompts they used, what the tool returned and what they changed. The facilitator reads it at each exit check.
- **Verify before use.** Any fact, figure, legal point or health claim from AI is traced to a source the learner has opened. Unsourced claims do not enter the Evidence Pack or the pitch.
- **Unaided checks.** Short checks with the tool closed take place at the start, the middle and the end of a cycle. They show whether the learner has learned or only produced.
- **Data stays out.** Learners never paste names, contact details or other personal information about community members into any AI tool.

Tool age limits and vendor terms are checked before each cohort, as set out in the Gate 2 age bands. Where a tool is not allowed for a learner's age, the facilitator runs it on screen and learners direct it.

## The toolkit

The toolkit has two parts: templates that are the same everywhere, and tool categories that change with the cohort. Templates are open and work on paper, so a site with no connectivity can run the full method.

### Templates by step

| Step | Template | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Listening Log | Records who said what, where, and the exact words used |
| 1 | Need Note | States the problem, who has it, and how they cope today |
| 2 | Evidence Pack | Holds interviews, observations, existing solutions and limits |
| 2 to 7 | Lens Sheet | Answers the five field-lens questions and is updated at each step |
| 3 | Options Board | Compares options against the evidence and constraints |
| 4 | Solution Spec | Defines users, one flow, data, AI and human roles, and the success measure |
| 4 to 6 | Do-No-Harm Check | Lists risks to users and community, with a fix and an owner for each |
| 5 | Build Log | Records prompts, versions, errors and fixes |
| 6 | Test Report | Records tests run, failures found and changes made |
| 7 | Pitch Outline and Business Case | Structures the argument and finishes the lens answers |
| 8 | Sustain Plan and Handover Agreement | Names the owner, costs, upkeep, the 90-day measure and the handover terms |
| Every cycle | Unaided Check | Short questions answered with the tool closed |
| Facilitators | Cycle Plan | Plans sessions, roles, partner visits and checks for one cycle |

### Tool categories by capability rung

The categories below follow the capability ladder from Gate 1. Brands change quickly, so each cohort picks current tools in these categories after checking cost, age access, security and offline options.

| Rung | Tool category | Low-connectivity option |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Chat and research | AI assistants and search | Small offline model, or the facilitator runs searches for the group |
| 2. Content and design | AI writing, image and layout tools | Paper layouts, drawn first and digitised later |
| 3. No-code and AI app builders | Visual and prompt-based app builders | Paper prototypes tested with users, then built on a shared device |
| 4. Automation and assistants | No-code automation and custom assistants | Rule-based scripts and message templates |
| 5. Code with AI help | AI coding assistants and editors | Offline editors with a small local model |
| 6. Deploy and publish | CMS such as WordPress, simple web hosting, messaging channels | Printed guides and messaging channels that need little data |
| 7. Operate and measure | Forms, spreadsheets and basic analytics | Paper tally sheets entered later |

(Note added when saving: the "CMS such as WordPress" example in rung 6 predates the Gate 7 redo. The BBC site itself uses no CMS, but learners' own projects may still use hosting or CMS tools.)

## Running one cycle

The facilitator plans the cycle with the Cycle Plan, books the community partner's visits at steps 1, 4, 7 and 8, makes the go, redesign or stop call at step 2, and signs off safety at step 6. Teams run the rest, and the three unaided checks show whether learning has kept pace with building.

Team roles are set in Gate 4, once the pilot cohort size is known.

## Safety and do-no-harm checks

The Studio puts learners in contact with real people and real data, so safety is built into the steps, not added at the end. The Do-No-Harm Check starts at step 4, is completed at step 6 and is signed off by the facilitator, never by the team.

The check asks five questions of every project:

1. **Who could be harmed** if the solution is wrong, misused or stops working?
2. **What data does it touch,** why is each item needed, and who can see it?
3. **What can AI get wrong here,** and how will users know and recover?
4. **What rules apply** to this field and place, such as professional, licensing and child-data rules?
5. **Who has agreed:** the partner, the users, and for minors a parent or educator?

Some projects cannot proceed at all, whatever the team's enthusiasm. A project stops or is redesigned if it gives medical, legal or financial advice that AI alone would produce, collects personal data it does not need, targets or profiles individuals, or puts a minor in direct contact with strangers.

Learners and community members can always withdraw. If a partner or user asks for their information or participation to be removed, the team removes it and records that it did.

The age bands from Gate 2 apply on top of these checks, and local counsel must confirm them in each pilot country.

## Decisions needed and next gate

Four decisions shape Gate 4, the flagship pilot curriculum. My recommendation is in bold.

1. **The AI use rules:** accept the three modes, including the ban on AI as author in steps 2, 7 and 8. **Recommended: yes.** It is the main protection against the failure mode in the Gate 1 evidence.
2. **The stop rules:** accept that projects giving AI-only medical, legal or financial advice, collecting unneeded personal data, or profiling individuals cannot proceed. **Recommended: yes.**
3. **Who the flagship pilot serves first:** the Build journey for learners aged 18 and over, with Explore run as a smaller, facilitator-led add-on once counsel clears the age rules in the pilot country. **Recommended:** this order, because it tests the method with the lowest child-data risk and still serves your pre-university goal soon after.
4. **Pilot format:** a facilitated cohort, in person or hybrid, not self-paced online. **Recommended: yes**, because Gate 1 named completion as the largest risk.

Still open from earlier gates and not blocking Gate 4: legal form, the three pilot contexts, a second delivery lead, and the domain and trademark checks. The pilot contexts do shape the Gate 4 detail, so naming even one now helps.
